# Hazardous Site Data

A primer for the contaminated site area-level enhancement data sets: Where contaminated site data come from, how its tables work, how to link them to further information, and what the known caveats are.
1. Purpose of this document
This is a working reference for anyone pulling or reviewing area-level Contaminated Site data. It's meant to sit alongside the variable summary workbook and answer the "why this table, why this caveat" questions before they come up individually. It's not a replacement for the EPA references linked in Section 6.
2. The EPA’s Databases
The Environmental Protection Agency’s (EPA) is the main data source for tracking contaminants such as “brownfields,” “superfund sites,” “toxic release institutions” (or TRIs), and other emissions. 
The Assessment, Cleanup, Redevelopment Exchange System (ACRES) database is the main source for brownfield data. Information about downloading ACRES data can be found here: https://www.epa.gov/cleanups/cimc-how-download-data. After downloading site information, one can click on “Click for More Brownfields Info” to download geographic information. We included the following characteristics: “Cooperative Agreement Recipient Information,” “Property Background Information,” “Property Geographic Information,” “Environment Assessment Information,” and “Contaminants and Media Affected Information.” 
Toxics Release Inventory (TRI) information can be downloaded here: https://www.epa.gov/toxics-release-inventory-tri-program/tri-basic-data-files-calendar-years-1987-present.
Superfund sites can be downloaded here: https://cumulis.epa.gov/supercpad/cursites/srchsites.cfm. 
The National Emissions Inventory can be found here: https://www.epa.gov/air-emissions-inventories/national-emissions-inventory-nei.
3. What is included
Brownfields are defined as “real property, the expansion, redevelopment, or reuse of which may be complicated by the presence or potential presence of a hazardous substance, pollutant, or contaminant.” Since 1995, the EPA’s Brownfields Program has tracked brownfield sites and provided grants and seed money to clean and redevelop these sites. Examples of brownfields include gas stations, factories, dry cleaning facilities, factories, landfills, ash ponds, and mining sites.  

Brownfields are generally commercial sites that may be contaminated with arsenic, asbestos, lead, petroleum and hydrocarbons, polycyclic aromatic hydrocarbons (PAHs), polychlorinated biphenyls (PCBs), volatile organic compounds, cadmium, chromium, dioxin, mercury, pesticides, or other contaminants. 

Superfund sites are defined as locations where “commercial or industrial wastes have been mismanaged and pose unacceptable risks to human health and the environment.” In 1980, Congress established the Comprehensive Environmental Response, Compensation, and Liability Act (CERCLA) with the purpose of cleaning up contaminated sites. CERCLA establishes “prohibitions and requirements” and assigns liability for closed or abandoned hazardous waste sites. When there is “no viable responsible party”, CERCLA provides funds – collected through taxes on chemical and petroleum industries – to clean up these sites, leading to its common name of “Superfund.” Examples of superfund sites include factories, military sites, mines, and oil spills.

In 1983, the EPA created the “National Priorities List” (NPL), which categorized 406 sites as the highest priority for cleanup (and likely most toxic sites). The NPL sites can qualify for funding for long-term remedial actions. The EPA also conducts a Hazardous Ranking System (HRS) which allows for identification of the most hazardous sites. 

The EPA’s Toxic Release Inventory (TRI) program documents pollutants for roughly 800 chemicals from operating factories. In certain sectors, facilities that release, treat, or recycle chemicals are required to report details on these chemicals to the EPA. Examples of TRI sites include manufacturing sites and natural gas processing facilities. The TRI program was established through the 1986 Emergency Planning and Community Right-to-Know Act, which provided a government mandate for public information on these hazards. 

TRI is a mandatory program for qualifying manufacturing sectors. TRI data are annual (collected in July each year), multimedia (reflecting chemical emissions to air, water, and land), and broad (capturing a wide array of pollutants). For more on TRI sites, see here. 

The National Emissions Inventory (NEI) measure air emissions from 1990 onward. These data are categorized into one of the following: 
•	Point sources, such as electric power plants, airports, and commercial facilities. 
•	Nonpoint sources, such as residential heating, asphalt paving, and solvent use.  
•	Onroad sources, including emissions from onroad vehicles. 
•	Nonroad sources, including construction equipment, lawn and garden equipment, trains and boats. 
•	Fire sources, including both wild and prescribed fires. 

For more on NEI sites, see here.

4. What isn’t included
The ACRES database identifies over 40,000 brownfield sites. However, there are an estimated 450,000 – 1 million brownfield sites in existence nationwide (see the EPA's Brownfield FAQs). The ACRES dataset only contains brownfields that have received federal funding for remediation efforts, which is a key limitation and selection issue of these data. 

The TRI data include facilities that produce at least 100 pounds of pollution. 

5. Temporal Availability and Caveats

The NEI is collected from states, localities, and tribal sources, which vary in what they consider point sources. For example, some states voluntarily provide data on sources such as dry cleaners, gas stations, and livestock facilities, which are generally captured as nonpoint sources. 

California has a different onroad pollution model than other states, which use the MOVES model.
